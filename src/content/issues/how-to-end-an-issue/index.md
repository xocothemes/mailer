There is a whole genre of advice about opening lines. Hook the reader. Start in the middle. Never begin with "I". Fine, all useful.

Almost nobody writes about endings, which is strange, because the ending is the part that does the work. It is where a reader decides whether to reply, whether to forward, and — quietly, without noticing — whether to open the next one.

Here is what I have learned from writing about three hundred of them badly.

## The four ways an issue ends badly

**It trails off.** You have made your point, you are tired, and you write "anyway, that's my two cents" and hit send. The reader feels the energy drop and closes the tab. Nothing bad happens. Nothing good happens either.

**It summarises.** "So to recap: authentication, hygiene, consistency." If the piece needed a recap, the piece was too long. A summary at the end of nine hundred words tells the reader you did not trust them to follow it.

**It asks for everything at once.** Reply, forward, share on social, reply to the survey, join the Discord, upgrade. Five asks is zero asks.

**It ends on a link.** Sending someone away in the final paragraph is the one genuinely costly mistake on this list, and it is the most common. Your last sentence is the most-read sentence in the issue after the subject line. Do not spend it on somebody else's website.

## What to do instead

Pick one of three endings and rotate them.

**The open question.** Not "what do you think?" — that is a non-question and gets non-answers. A real one, narrow enough to answer in a sentence: _what is the longest gap you have taken, and did the list survive it?_ You will get replies, and replies are the strongest deliverability signal you have.

**The forward-looking line.** One sentence about what is in the next issue, specific enough to be a promise. "Next time: what plain text actually does to your numbers." This is the closest thing to a retention lever that exists in this format, and it costs you eleven words.

**The turn.** Restate the argument in a way that is slightly sharper than how you opened it. Not a summary — a compression. The reader should feel that the piece arrived somewhere rather than stopping.

> The ending is where the reader decides what the issue was about. Whatever you put there is what they will remember, regardless of what you spent the middle on.

## The sign-off itself

Keep it the same every time. Readers use it as punctuation; changing it every issue is like changing your handwriting.

And put your actual email address under it. Not a form, not a "reply to this email" instruction — the address, written out. It reads as a person rather than a system, and about one reader a month will use it in a way that changes what you write next.
