Ask someone why they did not subscribe to a newsletter they were recommended and you rarely hear "the writing was bad". You hear "I could not tell what it was".

That is an archive problem.

## The signup page is the wrong place to explain yourself

A landing page says what you _intend_ to write. An archive shows what you _have_ written, which is a completely different claim and a much more credible one. Twelve titles in a row tell a reader the range, the frequency, the length, and whether you keep your promises — in about four seconds, with no copywriting required.

So the useful question is not "how do I write a better signup page". It is "can a stranger read my last ten issues without an account".

For a surprising number of newsletters, the answer is no. The archive is behind the provider's default template, on a subdomain nobody links to, sorted badly, with no excerpts.

## What a good archive does

Three things, in order of importance.

**It shows the shape.** Issue numbers, dates, titles, one line of what it was about. A reader should be able to scan thirty issues without scrolling past four of them. This is why a list beats a grid of cards here — cards are for browsing when you do not know what you want, and lists are for scanning when you do.

**It is honest about frequency.** If there is a nine-month gap in 2025, show it. Hiding dates does not make the gap smaller, it just makes the whole archive feel unmoored. People forgive gaps. They do not forgive finding out later.

**It is linkable.** Every issue on its own URL, with a title that makes sense out of context, and a preview image that is not your logo. Someone will link to one of these in a group chat eventually, and that link is worth more than any post you will write about yourself.

## The thing nobody does

Cross-link your own back issues inside new ones.

Not a "related posts" block at the bottom — those get ignored. I mean genuinely: when you make a claim you argued properly eight months ago, link the issue where you argued it. It does three jobs at once. It saves you re-explaining. It shows a new reader there is a body of work behind the sentence they are reading. And it moves people from one issue to two, which is the single biggest predictor of whether they stay.

> A reader who has read two of your issues is a subscriber. A reader who has read one is a visitor.

## A test you can run today

Open your archive in a private window. Give yourself thirty seconds. Then close it and write down:

- How often does this come out?
- What are the three things it is about?
- Which issue would you read first?

If you cannot answer all three, a stranger cannot either. The fix is almost never more writing. It is usually ordering, excerpts, and dates.
